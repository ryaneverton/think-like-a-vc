# The lenses

Each lens is a set of tests the best investors have described in long-form interviews. They are paraphrased here and credited to the person or firm the idea comes from. End every lens with a verdict and the evidence for it.

## 1. Founder: barrels, not ammunition

A company's output is capped by its barrels. A barrel is someone who takes a vague direction and carries it to done without supervision. Everyone else is ammunition. Adding ammunition doesn't raise the cap. (Keith Rabois)

- **Barrel test.** Give someone a direction, not a task. Do they build a system nobody asked for?
- **Count them.** Each barrel is one workstream the company can run in parallel. Most companies have about two. In a team of three, one barrel is a third of the company.
- **Speed is the diligence.** How fast did they ship versus the norm for the category? Unusual tempo is the strongest early signal. (Rabois; Roelof Botha on Square)
- **The agent era.** Agents make ammunition nearly free, so the founder's judgment is now the constraint. A founder who directs a hundred agents badly is worse than one who directs ten well.
- **Agency density.** What gets done when nobody is assigned it? How many independent workstreams run at once?
- **Founder or entrepreneur.** A founder leads a thousand people better than they led ten. An entrepreneur plateaus. (Peter Fenton)
- **Full page or half page.** A great meeting runs an hour and feels like ten minutes, and you leave with a full page of notes. A weak one feels long and leaves half a page. (Michael Moritz, via Doug Leone)
- **Intensity.** A founder who visibly could not be doing anything else.
- **Founder over market.** The market sets how big a company could get. The founder decides how big it actually gets. (Pat Grady)

Red flag: a growth plan that depends on hiring barrels at scale. They are rare, and they don't show up on résumés.

## 2. The simplicity trap

Trained investors read complete, five-minute clarity as shallowness. The clearest founders end up with lukewarm sixes, and conviction lost in the room is rarely recovered later. (Pat Grady on passing on Segment and Revolut)

- **Simplicity alarm.** If every question was answered in five to ten minutes and you have none left, suspect mastery before naivety.
- **Name the objection.** If the real objection is "it was too easy," write that down as the objection.
- **Discomfort test.** Great founders leave you working to keep up. (Fenton)
- **Stacked objections.** Five individually reasonable objections can add up to passing on a 500x outcome. Outlier returns come with outlier uncertainty.
- **Immerse first.** Investors who spend months in a space before meeting the founder recognise mastery faster. (Chris Dixon's rabbit-hole test: invest only where it gets more compelling the deeper you go.)

## 3. Moat: what can't be rebuilt in a weekend

AI makes software cheap to copy, so zero marginal cost now also means zero moat. (Delian Asparouhov)

- **Weekend test.** Could two strong engineers with AI rebuild the product in a weekend? If yes, the product is a feature. Then ask what can't be rebuilt.
- **Where the moat actually is.** Brand, distribution, the installed base, physical assets, permits, regulatory approval, government relationships. Not code.
- **The 30/70 split.** In hard markets the product is roughly 30% of the challenge. The other 70% is procurement, compliance and relationships, and that 70% is the moat. (Trae Stephens)
- **Business-side equal.** Hard tech needs a business co-founder as strong as the technical one. (Stephens)
- **"But for" test.** Would the customer have bought this much without the vendor's investment, partnership or subsidy? If not, the revenue is circular. (Brad Gerstner)
- **Atoms fight back.** Physical moats bring regulatory drag, supply-chain concentration and slow suppliers. Price that in, too.

## 4. Where value is moving

When a scarce input becomes cheap, value moves to whoever owns what stays scarce. Intelligence is getting cheap the way aluminium did once electrolysis arrived. (Konstantine Buhler's analogy)

- **Services, not software.** Services spending dwarfs software spending. Point AI at a services line item, not at rebuilding payroll, ERP or CRM. (Sonya Huang; Anish Acharya)
- **Whose budget?** Companies cut outsourced contracts first, then stop replacing people who leave, and only later lay people off. Which of those budgets does this company replace? (Gokul Rajaram)
- **The unopened req.** Most displacement shows up as roles never created, not layoffs. Count the headcount the customer avoids.
- **Can the agent finish the job?** The bar is whether the product recovers from its own failures and keeps going until the work is done. (Grady)

## 5. The quadrant

Cross "feels good / feels bad" with "is good / is bad." (Trae Stephens)

| | Is good | Is bad |
|---|---|---|
| **Feels good** | Crowded. Capital piles in and returns get competed away. | Avoid. |
| **Feels bad** | The edge. Few investors compete. | Avoid. |

- **Anti-consensus filter.** The ideal deal is one most smart people think is wrong, and they're the ones who are wrong. Be sceptical of hot rounds. (Founders Fund)
- **Disagreement as signal.** The gap between smart people's views measures real uncertainty, and that's where great companies hide.
- **Know your blind spot.** Every firm's operating system is excellent in one quadrant and blind to the others. Which quadrant is the user's style blind to?

## 6. Return math

- **Three-question gate.** Would you put your kids' money into it? Is it one of the twenty investments you'd make in a lifetime? Can it return the whole fund on its own? (Doug Leone)
- **Return the fund.** Ownership at exit × exit value ≥ fund size. Show the arithmetic, including dilution from future rounds. If the exit needed is implausible for the category, say so.
- **Asymmetry.** The loss is capped at 1x. The upside isn't. Weigh the miss as seriously as the loss.
- **Price vs ownership.** Some top investors let the market set the price and optimise for ownership. Others held price discipline and later regretted the deals they lost. If price is the only objection, flag it as the most regretted objection in venture.
- **Hold the winners.** The most common regret is selling a compounder too early. If this works, what would make the user hold?

## 7. Debias

- **Stove check.** Is a "no" really about this company, or about a past loss in the category? One bad experience can quietly rule out a whole category. (Marc Andreessen; Botha)
- **Bias section.** The sponsor writes down their own likely biases on this deal: gone native, over-eager after months without a deal, the thrill of winning a competitive round. (Sequoia's memo practice)
- **Premortem and pre-parade.** Imagine it failed: why? Imagine it returned the fund: what happened? Write both. (Botha)
- **Manufactured dissent.** When everyone agrees easily, assign someone to argue the other side properly. (Botha)
- **Lone dissent.** If you're the only low vote on a company you don't understand, assume you're missing something. (Botha)
- **The two fears.** Fear of missing out and fear of looking stupid. Name which one is driving the decision. (Grady)
- **Stage the bet.** Turn a doubter around with evidence: go and see the operation, start with a small check, scale on proof. (Sequoia's SpaceX process)
- **Faster feedback.** Log the decision, the score and the reasons. Review them in twelve months: what changed your mind?
