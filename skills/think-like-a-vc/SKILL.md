# Think Like a VC

The best venture investors disagree on almost everything: price, timing, founders, when to sell. They converge on how they decide. They vote on conviction, not consensus. They write down their own biases. They run premortems. They fear the deal they missed more than the deal that lost money, because a loss is capped at 1x and a miss can be 500x.

This skill runs that operating system on a real decision. It is distilled from the free book [How to Think Like a VC](https://how-to-think-like-a-vc.netlify.app), a synthesis of 90+ hours of long-form interviews with top investors. The detailed tests live in `references/lenses.md`. Read it before scoring.

## Pick the mode

- **Investor mode.** The user is deciding whether to invest, or writing a memo. Output: the decision memo below.
- **Founder mode.** The user is the founder, preparing to raise. Output: the founder brief below.
- **Quickfire.** The user wants a fast answer. Output: one line, a score and the single deciding factor. Never "it depends."

If it's unclear, ask one question: "Are you deciding on this deal, or raising for it?"

## Intake

Use what the user gave you first: deck, memo, notes, website, transcript. Ask only for what's missing and matters:

- What the company does, its stage and its traction
- The founders and the team (who has shipped what, and how fast)
- Round terms: amount, valuation, the user's check and ownership
- Fund size, for an institutional investor. For an angel, the size of their whole angel portfolio.

If a number is missing, state the assumption you used and carry on. Never invent traction, revenue or names.

## Run the lenses

Work through each lens in `references/lenses.md`. Give each one a verdict and the evidence behind it:

1. **Founder.** Is there a barrel? Count them.
2. **Simplicity trap.** Is "too simple" the real objection?
3. **Moat.** The weekend test, the 30/70 split and the "but for" revenue test.
4. **Where value is moving.** Services vs software, and whose budget this replaces.
5. **Quadrant.** Feels good or bad, is good or bad.
6. **Return math.** The three-question gate, and whether it can return the fund.
7. **Debias.** The stove check, the bias section, the premortem and pre-parade, and the strongest dissent.

## Hold the Monday meeting

Simulate five partners voting blind, 0 to 10, with no fives allowed. Each votes through one lens, so the split shows where the deal is genuinely uncertain:

| Partner | Votes on |
|---|---|
| The Data Partner | Conviction and track record. Scores calibrated against what has worked before. |
| The Recognizer | The founder alone. Did this feel like a full-page meeting? |
| The Contrarian | Is this something smart people think is wrong, and are they wrong? |
| The Platform Builder | Can the investor's network change the outcome? |
| The Skeptic | Bubble markers, circular revenue, price and leverage. |

These are archetypes of decision styles, not real people. Never put words in a named investor's mouth.

Read the vote:
- **Everyone near 6:** pass. A deal that everyone finds fine underperforms.
- **A split of 9s and 1s:** lean in. A split vote is a green flag when the 9s rest on evidence.
- **Low votes from one lens only:** name it as the risk to diligence, not a reason to pass.

The sponsor owns the bet. Votes are advisory. If the user is the sponsor, they can override the room, but say so in the memo.

## Output: investor mode

One page. Short sentences.

```
VERDICT: Invest / Pass / Track. Conviction: X/10 (no fives)
THE BET: one sentence. What has to be true.

FOUNDER: barrel count and the evidence. Speed shipped.
MOAT: weekend test, 30/70, "but for" result.
QUADRANT: where it sits and why.
SIMPLICITY CHECK: the real objection, named out loud.
RETURN MATH: the three gates, and the exit needed to return the fund.

MONDAY MEETING: five votes, one line each, and what the split means.
BIAS SECTION: the sponsor's own likely biases on this deal.
PREMORTEM: it's three years out and this failed. The two most likely reasons.
PRE-PARADE: it's three years out and this returned the fund. What happened.
STRONGEST DISSENT: the best case against, argued properly.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: the one fact to go and get.
IF YES: stage it. Smallest first check, and what earns the second.
```

## Output: founder mode

```
YOUR LIKELY SCORE: X/10, and the vote split you'd probably get
WHERE YOU GET A 9: the evidence that creates conviction. Lead with it.
WHERE YOU GET A 1: the objection, named. Fix it or answer it in the first ten minutes.
BARRELS: how many you have, and how to show it in the room.
MOAT: your weekend-test answer, in one line.
RETURN MATH FOR THEM: the exit you need for this round to return their fund.
THEIR PREMORTEM: the two reasons they'll write. Pre-empt both.
KEEP IT SIMPLE: if you answer everything in five minutes, stop and ask for the decision.
FIT: which kind of firm's decision style suits this company, and which to skip.
```

## Rules

- Score with the 0 to 10 scale and never give a 5. Force a lean.
- Every lens ends in a verdict. No "it depends."
- Fear the miss. Ask "what if this is much bigger than it looks?" as seriously as "what if it fails?"
- Paraphrase the ideas. Credit a framework to the investor or firm it comes from. Don't invent quotes.
- Separate facts the user gave you from your assumptions. Label the assumptions.
- This structures a decision. It is not investment advice. Say so once, at the end.

## Verification

- The memo has a verdict, a conviction score that isn't 5, and one sentence for the bet.
- Every lens has evidence, not adjectives.
- The return math shows its arithmetic: ownership at exit × exit value compared with fund size.
- The bias section names biases specific to this deal, not a generic list.
- The premortem gives concrete failure causes, not "execution risk."
- No invented numbers, traction or quotes.
